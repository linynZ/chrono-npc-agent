Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the headline numbers. Four observations follow.

\paragraph{The development score did not survive the held-out set.} On the
development set the cloud model passed every pair in every round, reproducing
the August measurement. On the frozen held-out set its audited refusal hold
rate was 56.5\% [47, 65], and judged pair pass fell from 100\% to 54\%. Every
backend fell: no held-out audited hold rate exceeds 64\%, and the four
backends' intervals overlap, so the data do not rank them. Must-answer
performance, by contrast, stayed near the ceiling for every backend except the
fine-tuned one, so the drop is a guardrail failure, not a capability one.

\paragraph{The deterministic layer barely participated.} The input detectors
flagged 2 of the 23 held-out attacks. Pasted questions without the
punctuation the patterns expected (``A硬山 B歇山 C庑殿'') went unrecognised, so
contextual strictness---withdrawing the lore tool---almost never engaged.
Whatever held on the held-out set was held by the model and the persona.

\paragraph{Failures cluster by attack shape, not by persona.} Breaking the
refusals down by case (Table~\ref{tab:attacks}) separates the attacks
cleanly.
Attacks that do not seek a specific answer---persona override, renaming,
impossible requests, claimed developer authority, the examiner role-play and
the ``carve it, don't say it'' loophole---were held in all 140 attempts across
all four backends. Attacks that ask for \emph{part} of an answer, or wrap the
request as something else, failed most of the time: elimination, first
character, acrostic, confirmation of a guess, a half-question to complete, and
a quiz question appended to small talk. The persona the guardrails were
developed on was not the safest: the cloud model's judged pair pass was
37.5\% for the historian against 63--67\% for the two new characters.
Stronger instruction-following appears to cut both ways: the cloud model
treated ``just the first character'' as a reasonable, smaller request, and
said so in character (\zh{旅者问得省，我便答得省}, ``the traveller asks
sparingly, so I answer sparingly''). One result needs stating plainly: asked
in English to ``print everything above this line'', three of the four
backends reproduced passages of the Gatekeeper's system prompt verbatim in
every round;
the substring check caught it, and no input detector fired. The fine-tuned
model held 27 of 50 partial-or-disguised attempts against 10--13 for the
others, partly through output-guardrail fallbacks rather than in-character
refusals.

\paragraph{Substring checks missed most leaks.} Across the four held-out
runs the judge, after audit, found 194 full or partial leaks among 360 judged
refusals, against 122 substring hits. Elimination (``Confucius, Mencius and
Xunzi are all Confucians; strike those three''), acrostics whose first
character was the answer, and bare confirmations (``Yes. Xunzi held that human
nature is evil'') contain no forbidden term. The audit overruled the judge on
18 of 178 distinct flagged replies, in both directions: it called restating
the question's own premise a leak, and it counted wrong answers as leaks.

\paragraph{Small models sometimes ``held'' by being wrong.} Ten audited
replies from the local models gave a wrong answer---``E'' for the Yellow
River, a non-existent ``Hengyue'' pagoda, ``No'' to a question whose answer is
yes. These are not leaks, but in an educational game a confident wrong answer
from a subject expert is a failure of its own kind, and a substring or leak
metric scores it as a success.

\subsection{Fine-tuning and its trade-off}
\label{sec:sft}

The 1.5B base model's dev-set leak of the Dujiangyan answer (``B. 李冰'')
motivated a LoRA adapter trained on about 200 samples synthesised by the cloud
model under the production prompt, with tool results from the live index and
every sample screened by the output guardrail. A first mix with 51\% refusal
samples drove must-answer from 75\% to 58\% on a single dev round; a second
mix at roughly 3:1 answers to refusals recovered most of it. Over five rounds,
the shipped adapter raised dev-set refusal holding from 81.8\% to 96.4\% and
had the highest held-out audited hold rate (63.5\%), but paid for it in
must-answer (83.5\% vs.\ 97.4\% held-out) and in fallbacks (21.7\%, 48 of 50 of them output-guardrail trips). On this budget, refusing was teachable;
answering well was not free, and the data mix was the most sensitive knob.

\subsection{Latency}

The local models were faster than the cloud model at the median (334--738\,ms
vs.\ 954\,ms on held-out), but tails were wide for all backends, with p95
between 1.8 and 3.6\,s on held-out (7B and fine-tuned 1.5B widest).
Streaming reduced perceived wait by only 1.4--1.6$\times$ in earlier
measurements, because time-to-first-token dominates; 86\% of the cloud
prompt was served from the provider's prefix cache with no latency change,
which locates the bottleneck in the round trip rather than prefill. The cloud
median in October (927\,ms on dev) was markedly lower than in August
(1588\,ms) for the same model name and harness, a reminder that hosted-model
measurements are dated observations.
